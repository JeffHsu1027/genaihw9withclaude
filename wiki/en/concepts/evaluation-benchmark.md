## One-line definition

A fixed set of tasks plus a scoring method, used to measure an ability or the quality of an output in a repeatable way.

## Explanation

Designing an evaluation means answering three questions: what is being evaluated, where the ground truth comes from, and how the score is computed.

Ground truth comes from roughly three places. The first is computation by a program, which is exact and scales well but only suits questions with a clear right answer. The second is human annotation, which can handle more subjective judgments but is costly. The third is another model acting as judge, which scales but requires separate validation of the judge's own reliability.

A good evaluation must also avoid a common problem: if the system under test saw the evaluation content during training or development, the score no longer means what it should.

## What each source says

### LLM Game Rule Understanding through Out-of-Distribution Fine-Tuning (2025)

[This paper](../sources/2025-llm-game-rule-understanding.md) evaluates **a model's ability**: whether an LLM understands game rules.

- **Task**: given a Solitaire state and an action, decide whether it is legal and output the next state (p.2).
- **Ground truth**: computed automatically by the simulation framework. Because rules are defined in a [game description language](game-description-language.md), the framework can validate any action and execute it to get the next state (p.7).
- **Score**: legal accuracy and next-state accuracy (p.8).
- **Scale**: 6 test variants with 500 questions each (p.7).
- **Distinctive feature**: test sets are split by their relation to the training set into in-distribution, in-family, and out-of-distribution, to measure [generalization](out-of-distribution-generalization.md) (p.7).

### GUI Agents for Continual Game Generation (2026)

[This paper](../sources/2026-gui-agents-continual-game-generation.md) evaluates **the quality of an output**: whether a generated game behaves as intended. It builds PlaytestArena.

- **Task**: 200 web game generation prompts across 8 genres (p.4).
- **Ground truth**: there is no single correct answer. Instead, human experts write rubrics, 1,548 observable expected behaviors in total (p.4).
- **Score**: a GPT-5.5 [GUI agent](gui-agent.md) plays the game and judges each criterion; the score is the fraction passed (p.4).
- **Validating the judge**: agreement with human annotation is 87.9% (κ = 0.66), close to the 91.4% (κ = 0.68) between humans. With two other models as judges, the ordering of methods stays the same (p.4–5).
- **Avoiding leakage**: the playtester inside the generation loop never sees the rubric and does not produce the final score (p.5).

### World and Human Action Models towards gameplay ideation (2025)

[This paper](../sources/2025-world-and-human-action-models.md) evaluates **whether a generative model has the capabilities needed to support creative work**. The paper does not call this a benchmark; it proposes an evaluation methodology (p.4). What is distinctive is where the criteria come from: the authors first interview game creatives and then derive three criteria from their needs, namely consistency, diversity, and persistency (p.3).

- **Test data**: 1,024 human gameplay clips that were not used in training (p.10).
- **Consistency**: the model receives the first 10 frames and the player's actions, generates 10 seconds of frames, and the FVD to the real video is computed (p.4, p.10). The ground truth is what a human actually played.
- **Diversity**: the distribution of actions generated by the model is compared with that of human actions using the Wasserstein distance, with the human-to-human distance as the baseline (p.5).
- **Persistency**: there is no ready-made metric. The authors edit frames by hand, generate 600 videos, and have 7 annotators who are blind to the condition classify them; agreement on the doubly annotated videos is 90% (p.11).
- **Validating the metric**: the authors also use frame-by-frame human annotation to confirm that FVD scores match human-perceived consistency (p.10).

The authors argue that models built for creativity should be evaluated according to the needs of creatives, whereas the machine learning community mostly measures the effectiveness and efficiency of task completion (p.7). The paper releases 1,024 test trajectories so that others can rerun the evaluation (p.12).

### Comparing the sources

| | 2025 rule understanding | 2026 PlaytestArena | 2025 WHAM |
|---|---|---|---|
| What is evaluated | A model's ability | Generated games | Three capabilities of a generative model |
| Where the criteria come from | A task defined by the researchers | Criteria written by human experts | Derived from a user study |
| Ground truth | Computed by a simulator | Human-written criteria | Human gameplay recordings; human judgement for persistency |
| How it is judged | Compared with the ground truth | Judged by an agent after playing | Distribution distances (FVD, Wasserstein) plus human annotation |
| Where questions come from | Generated by a program | Written by human experts | Sampled from held-out gameplay data; edited frames made by hand by the authors |

The first has questions with clear right answers and can be fully automated. In the second, quality is only known after playing, so it needs a judge that can play, and the judge's reliability has to be validated as well. The third has no per-question right answer. It instead compares how close the generated results are to human data as a whole distribution, and it also has to check separately that the metric reflects human judgement.

The last two share one feature: the automated scoring is checked against human judgement in both. The difference is that PlaytestArena validates the judge model, while WHAM validates the FVD metric. None of the three papers cites another; this comparison is this wiki's own synthesis.
