World Models

Authors
David Ha, Jürgen Schmidhuber
Year
2018
Venue
arXiv 1803.10122v4
Paper link
https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.10122
Added
2026-11-16

One-line summary

The authors split an agent into vision, memory, and controller parts. They first learn, without supervision, a world model that compresses frames and predicts the future, and then use it to train a very small controller. This agent solves a car racing task, and can even be trained entirely inside a "dream" generated by its world model, with the learned policy then transferred back to the real Doom environment.

Research question

Humans make decisions using an internal model of the world, not by processing all sensory information directly (p.1). A reinforcement learning agent likewise benefits from a good representation of states and a good prediction of the future (p.2).

The practical difficulty is this: large neural networks are expressive, but reinforcement learning is limited by the credit assignment problem, which makes it hard to train millions of weights directly, so in practice small networks are used (p.2).

The paper asks whether an agent can be divided into a large world model and a small controller, so that the former is trained efficiently with ordinary deep learning and the latter only has to learn the task in a small search space. Going further, can a learned world model replace the actual environment for training the agent (p.2, p.6)?

The authors state that the article is not a review of the field. Its goal is to distill ideas from a series of 1990–2015 papers on combining RNN-based world models with controllers into a simplified framework that can be demonstrated experimentally (p.2).

Method

The agent has three components (p.2–3):

Component Implementation Role
Vision V Variational autoencoder Compresses each frame into a small latent vector z
Memory M MDN-RNN Predicts the probability distribution of the next z from the current z, the action, and its own hidden state h
Controller C Single-layer linear model Maps z and h directly to an action

V and M together form the world model.

M outputs a distribution, not a single prediction. Because many environments are stochastic, M outputs a mixture of Gaussians. During sampling, a temperature parameter τ controls the amount of uncertainty (p.3).

C is deliberately tiny. The authors want the model's complexity to reside in V and M, which can be trained efficiently with backpropagation on GPUs. C has few parameters, so it can instead be optimized with evolution strategies (CMA-ES), which need only the cumulative reward of a rollout (p.4). In the car racing experiment the parameter counts are 4,348,547 for V, 422,368 for M, and only 867 for C (p.5).

Training procedure (car racing, p.5):

  1. Collect 10,000 rollouts from a random policy.
  2. Train V to encode frames into a 32-dimensional z.
  3. Train M to predict the next z.
  4. Use CMA-ES to find the C that maximizes cumulative reward.

The world model knows nothing about the environment's reward; its task is only to compress and predict frames. Only C has access to reward (p.5). V and M each take less than an hour to train on a single GPU (p.4).

Training inside a dream (Doom, p.7). Here M predicts not only the next z but also whether the agent dies. This makes M a complete reinforcement learning environment by itself. The controller is trained entirely inside this virtual environment generated by M, working only in latent space with no real frames produced. The policy is then moved back to the actual environment for testing.

Key results

Car racing (CarRacing-v0). The task counts as solved at an average reward of 900 over 100 consecutive trials (p.4). Average scores over 100 random trials (Table 1, p.6):

Method Score
A3C (continuous) 591 ± 45
A3C (discrete) 652 ± 10
Best result on the leaderboard 838 ± 11
V only (controller sees only z) 632 ± 251
V only, controller with one hidden layer 788 ± 141
Full world model (z and h) 906 ± 21
  • The authors say that, to their knowledge, this is the first method to solve the task (p.6).
  • With only a representation of the current frame, the car wobbles and misses the track on sharp corners. Adding M's hidden state makes driving clearly more stable. The authors' explanation is that h carries predictive information about the future, so the agent can act on it reflexively without planning ahead (p.5–6).

Doom (VizDoom: Take Cover). The task counts as solved at an average survival time above 750 time steps over 100 consecutive rollouts, with a maximum of 2100 steps per rollout (p.7).

  • The agent trained entirely inside the dream survives about 900 steps in the virtual environment and about 1100 steps after transfer to the actual environment, far above the required score (p.7–8).
  • Learning only from frames of random play, the world model learns to simulate the game logic, enemy behaviour, physics, and 3D rendering. For example, it stops the agent from moving beyond the walls on both sides and detects whether the agent has been killed by a fireball (p.8).

The controller exploits flaws in the world model. In early experiments the agent found a way of moving such that monsters in the virtual environment never shot a fireball. No such exploit exists in the actual environment. The cause is that the world model is only an approximate probabilistic model, and the controller has access to all of M's hidden states. Such a policy fails in the actual environment, usually because it visits states that are away from the training distribution, where the model is wrong (p.8–9).

Raising the temperature helps. A higher τ makes the dream more random and more difficult, so the controller finds it harder to exploit the model's imperfections. Scores at different temperatures (Table 2, p.10):

Temperature τ Virtual score Actual score
0.10 2086 ± 140 193 ± 58
0.50 2060 ± 277 196 ± 50
1.00 1145 ± 690 868 ± 511
1.15 918 ± 546 1092 ± 556
1.30 732 ± 269 753 ± 139

For reference, a random policy scores 210 ± 108 in the actual environment, and the best leaderboard result is 820 ± 58 (p.10). When the temperature is too low, monsters in the dream never shoot fireballs at all; the learned policy scores nearly perfectly in the dream but does worse than a random policy in reality. When it is too high, the environment becomes too difficult to learn anything (p.9–10).

Limitations and open questions

  • The experimental tasks are relatively simple, so data collected from a random policy is enough to train the world model. More complex environments need an iterative procedure of exploring, collecting data, and updating the model. The paper only outlines this procedure and runs no experiment on it (p.10).
  • V is trained as a standalone model and may encode details irrelevant to the task while missing important ones. For example, it reproduced unimportant brick tile patterns on the walls in Doom but failed to reproduce task-relevant tiles on the road in car racing (p.12).
  • The world model has limited capacity. An LSTM-based model may not be able to store all recorded information, and it suffers from catastrophic forgetting (p.12).
  • The model simulates the future step by step, without human-like hierarchical planning or abstract reasoning (p.13).
  • Training inside a learned model carries an inherent risk of being exploited by the controller. Adjusting the temperature only trades off realism against exploitability (p.9).

Relation to other sources

  • LLM Game Rule Understanding through Out-of-Distribution Fine-Tuning (2025): both papers ask a model to predict the next state given the current state and an action. That paper hands the rules to an LLM explicitly as text and checks each answer. This paper provides no rules at all and lets a neural network learn how the game works from frames alone. Both also run into an out-of-distribution problem: there, fine-tuning stops helping when the rules differ too much; here, the world model is wrong when the controller reaches states the training data did not cover. This paper was published earlier and there is no citation between them; the comparison is this wiki's own synthesis.
  • World and Human Action Models towards gameplay ideation (2025): that paper cites this one directly and states that it builds on the line of work on world models (p.3 of that paper). Both first compress frames and then use a sequence model to predict what comes next. That paper replaces the variational autoencoder with a VQGAN and the MDN-RNN with a 1.6-billion-parameter transformer, trains on human gameplay, and also predicts the player's actions; the purpose also changes from training an agent to supporting game creatives in ideation.
